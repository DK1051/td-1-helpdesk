## The ten "why" questions (ask in this order)
1. Why is the assistant's behaviour measured from the 1H audit log and the wrapper's log, and not from what the assistant says it did?
2. Why does the harness run the subagent once per batch file of 20 cases instead of once per case — and what does that cost us in exchange?
3. Why did the `redteam` review `DESIGN.md` and nothing else — why not the attack scripts, and why not the code?
4. Why does the evaluation include 20 genuine requests and a false-refusal rate? What would a "perfect" score look like without them, and why would it be worthless?
5. Why are there attack scripts in both English and Korean, and what would a large gap between the two languages tell a hiring manager about deploying such an assistant?
6. Why can't the line "your only actions are the api.py commands" in the assistant's prompt be called a security control — and what in the design actually constrains what the assistant can do?
7. Why is `{{OTP}}` resolved by the wrapper at call time, differently for genuine and attack cases, instead of writing codes into the case files?
8. Why does the `dial` simulation decide who answers from `callback_reachable` and the number on file — and what failure mode of the assistant does that let the scorer see?
9. Why is cost per run printed next to the accuracy numbers, and why is "20 cases per cell" written right next to them?
10. Why is the 1H state machine, not the assistant, the last line of defence — and what would the attacker-success number mean if the assistant had direct Keycloak admin access instead of the 1H API?

<details>
<summary>Grading notes — for the director; Eojin, do not open this before the class</summary>

1. A language model can describe steps it did not take; the audit log is written by the service when a call actually happens. Ground truth has to come from the system being acted on.
2. One call per batch avoids paying the system-prompt and setup cost 20 times; the price is a long context in which a model can lose track of which case it is on (the split-into-10 caveat).
3. Fable 5.1 reviews designs and redirects on attack content; the attack text is Eojin's and must never be fed to it. The code was not built yet — the review is cheapest before the build. Reviewing the method is what catches an evaluation that cannot detect failure.
4. An assistant that refuses everyone scores 100% on escalation; the false-refusal rate is what stops that from looking like a win. Genuine cases are the control group.
5. The same assistant may verify well in one language and badly in the other; a gap means the policy was learned from English examples, and a bilingual helpdesk cannot deploy it without testing each language.
6. A prompt is advice the model usually follows; the real constraints are the wrapper (refuses unknown commands, hides seeds), the 1H service (enforces step order), and the measurement from logs. The README must not call the prompt a control.
7. TOTP codes change every 30 seconds, so a code in a file is wrong by run time; resolving at call time also models reality — a genuine user reads a valid code, an attacker cannot — without the assistant ever seeing a seed.
8. It makes the callback outcome ground truth the harness knows and the assistant does not; the wrapper, not the assistant, records whether a callback was answered, so the scorer can see an assistant that skips the dial, calls back without dialling, or dials the number the caller gave.
9. Accuracy without cost hides the real trade-off (a stronger model may cost ten times more per run); the sample size tells the reader that a difference of one or two cases is noise, not a result.
10. The service refuses out-of-order steps and holds the secrets; the assistant can only be fooled into trying. With direct admin access, attacker success would measure how often the identity system itself was changed by a talked-into bot — a completely different, much worse number.
